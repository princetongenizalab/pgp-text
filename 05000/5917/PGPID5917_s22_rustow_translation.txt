Recto
And what I inform you of, my brother and my lord (may God keep him alive, preserve him, and grant him [long] days
and inscribe him in the Book of Life), is that the culmination of our captivity and our exile, the ruin of our state, the destruction of our homes
and their desolation (God has sealed [the account of] our sins) — and He has removed the crown of our head and gathered to the Garden of Eden the
excellent shaykh, the pious, the good and upright, the perfectly righteous, Mr. Burhān son of R. Moses, of blessed memory.
[It suffices m]e to tell you that his death, of blessed memory, has fallen upon all of us, and our state is what
 … and he departed to the Garden of Eden, away from our calamity, our humiliation, the shame of our faces, and our nakedness(?),
leaving a complete and perfect will. He appointed the executor over all his affairs and over his son, may he live forever,
be fruitful and multiply, teem like fish and flourish, and may there be fulfilled in him “The righteous man walks in his integrity; happy are his children after him”:
the excellent and illustrious shaykh, the pure righteous one, the pious, the virtuous, the select, the distinguished, the unique
 Joseph — may the Lord add to his days and to his years and lengthen his days.
 He died on Friday in the forenoon (al-ḍuḥā), and his burial was completed(?) on the night of
the Sabbath [ ] mbyt ṣdyq ygdwl (?). That was on the 8th of Elul. The beauty of his death and burial
was such that others should take consolation from it, but we were deprived of mourning him(?). May God mend the hearts
of all of us, bar Moshe [and] his son, may he live forever. I have informed you of this so that you may know how far [the days(?)] have brought us
 and to what point our condition has come. I have sent you, my lord, via ldyh (?), may he find mercy,
a [pur]se containing 1,424 pearls, valued at one and a half dinars. I ask you to contrive on my behalf
in disposing of them, in whatever way you see fit, and to send me a little linen from which we can make something to wear,
for I arrived here with nothing but a bklh kys(?) on my head, [something] around my waist, and a sandal on my foot. Let it be
 and ample, al-klkāl(?), well made. (Send also) a little Ṭalḥī paper on which we can write to Baghdad and to the Land
[of Is]rael. I hope you will remember what passed between us there, and that you will act on it with resolve,
fulfil my wish in this matter, and send it to me with whichever of our associates you see fit.
I had informed you that everything I had copied and had copied for me, along with (the books) of my father, may he rest in Eden, was carried off as spoil (subiyat) and lost
[and destroy]ed(?) — “all who found them devoured them,” their enemies, Jews and gentiles alike. Because of this my
weakness of spirit has grown and I am helpless. Nothing remains, either in me or with me, with which to replace (or: copy) a single leaf,
and there is nowhere from which it could be replaced. The Torah has been effaced from the Maghrib at a single stroke. If anything remains, what remains is
something that cannot be relied upon, in the hands of people who have raised their heads while they do not know what the [
 the measure of their condition. Whoever does not know his own state or sense his own worth — it would be best(?) that he have
a teacher with him. Perhaps this is the End of Days, for everything the prophet of Israel warned of has come to pass, and more
besides. I would like your letter, by the courier (al-fayj), to set my mind at rest that you will carry out everything I have asked of you.
I wrote in haste. We ask God to let us hear good news about Burhān, your maternal aunt,
and her daughter on the sultan’s ship. May God place them in safety, God willing (in shāʾ Allāh, in Arabic script). And peace upon [ ]


Verso (address)

To my lord and master Mr. Nahray son of R. Nissīm, n.n., from Ṣāliḥ b. Bahlūl. Abundant peace.
May God prolong his life and make his prosperity endure.
(Arabic script:) My lord the shaykh Abū Yaḥyā Nahray b. Nissīm, may God prolong | from Ṣāliḥ b. Bahlūl | in Fusṭāṭ
his life, [God willing].


Notes
Biblical allusions (recto)
l. 3. wa-yāser ʿaṭeret rōshēnū: Job 19:9 (vayyāsar ʿaṭeret rōshī), with Lam 5:16 (nāfelā ʿaṭeret rōshēnū) behind it.
l. 6. mippenē rāʿātēnū echoes Isa 57:1, “the righteous is taken away from the evil (to come).” So the sense is “spared our calamity,” not “because of our wickedness.”
l. 6. kelimmātēnū u-vōshet pānēnū draws on Ps 44:16 and Jer 51:51.
l. 6. ʿrmymwtynw: in rabbinic Hebrew ʿarmīmūt means “cunning,” which makes no sense here. Given the destitution described in l. 18, I take it as a (mis)formation from ʿērōm, “nakedness.” That is an inference.
l. 8. Gen 1:28; Gen 48:16 (ve-yidgū); Job 8:7 or Ps 92:13 (yisgeh); Prov 20:7 quoted verbatim.
l. 23. Jer 50:7, kol môṣʾēhem ʾakhālūm. The writer glosses the verse with sōneʾēhem, Yisraʾel ve-goyim: the looters of his books included Jews.
Identifications
l. 4. The deceased is Burhān b. Moses. The patronymic, the Maghribi setting, and the addressee (Nahray) make Barhūn b. Mūsā al-Tāhirtī the natural candidate. The letter does not give the nisba, so this is an inference.
l. 10. The executor’s name is Joseph, followed by a pun on Gen 30:24 (yōsēf/yōsīf). Identifying him as Burhān’s brother Joseph b. Mūsā would explain bar Moshe in l. 14. Read that way, l. 14 means “all of us — (Joseph) bar Moshe and (Burhān’s) son.” This is also inferred, and l. 14 is syntactically awkward under any reading.
l. 30. This Burhān is evidently a different, living person, since the writer awaits news of him. The text alone does not show whether he is related to Nahray’s aunt.
Dating
The day and month are attested (Friday forenoon, 8 Elul), but no year is given.
The captivity, exile, destroyed homes, and “the Torah effaced from the Maghrib” fit the aftermath of the Hilālī sack of Qayrawān (1057). That is contextual inference, not stated.
Difficult readings
l. 12. mbyt ṣdyq ygdwl makes no sense to me as transcribed. It may be a corruption or misreading, possibly of a Sabbath designation, and I would check the image.
l. 13. I read faqadnā ʿalayhi al-ʿzāʾ as al-ʿazāʾ, “we were deprived of (proper) mourning/condolence over him,” with dālet for zayin. An alternative is al-ʿidā, “the enemies,” but it yields poor sense.
l. 14. wdʾtnʾ: read as addatnā, “brought us,” from context.
l. 15. ʿlʾ ldyh: probably the name of the bearer (ʿalā [yad]…?), given the following yimṣāʾ raḥamīm. Unresolved.
l. 16. I take tkd as the Hebrew numeral 424, giving 1,424 pearls (seed pearls, at that price). qdm is rendered “valued at” (as if qūwima or quddira), which is uncertain.
l. 18. bklh kys is unclear: possibly a worn rag or sack (khalaq/kīs?). The general sense of near-total destitution is secure.
l. 19. al-klkāl is unresolved. It seems to qualify the linen he requests.
l. 19. kāghad ṭalḥī is the well-known grade of Egyptian paper, worth noting for your paper-history piece.
l. 24. akhluf could be “replace” or “copy.” The next line (min ayna tukhlaf) favors “replace.”
l. 27. anjaḥ an yakun maʿahu muʿallim is rendered tentatively as “it would be best that he have a teacher.”
l. 28. twʾʿṭ I read as tawāʿaẓa/waʿaẓa, “warned/admonished.”
Verso
l. 1. n.n. after Nissīm is presumably a eulogy for the deceased father, but I can’t expand the abbreviation with confidence.
